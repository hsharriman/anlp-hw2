ABOUT THE
Bach Choir of Pittsburgh was founded in 1934 to exclusively perform
works of Johann Sebastian Bach. The repertoire soon expanded to
include other composers and has even included music commissioned
by the Bach Choir. Under the leadership of Conductor and Artistic
Director Thomas W. Douglas, the Choir continues to evolve,
becoming more inclusive, innovative, and diverse. Today, the Choir
performs choral works by composers from the Renaissance
through the present. The 2026-2027 Season celebrates the
culmination of Thomas Douglas’s remarkable twenty-three year
tenure with the Bach Choir.
Mission DONATE
The mission of the Bach Choir of Pittsburgh
is to perform innovative choral music
for arts-loving audiences by presenting
unique artistic experiences drawn from
a variety of musical genres and diverse
cultural traditions.

ARTIST PROFILES
THOMAS W. DOUGLAS
Artistic Director
Thomas Douglas is a compelling
and passionate conductor, having
performed more than 250 opera,
oratorio, orchestral and musical theater
productions. As the Artistic Director
of the Bach Choir of Pittsburgh he
regularly receives accolades for
innovative, creative, and dramatically
provocative productions. He was
also the music director of the Newton
Symphony in Kansas. Career highlights
include conducting Webber’s Phantom
of the Opera in Basel, Switzerland,
Gershwin’s Porgy and Bess, the
Pittsburgh premiere of the classic silent film The Passion of Joan of
Arc, with live orchestra and chorus, the U.S. premiere of David Chesky’s
The Agnostic, the rousing Carmina Burana Africana, Mozart’s Die
Zauberflöte and Don Giovanni and a production of Ain’t Misbehavin’ in
Malaysia.
Thomas works regularly as Musical Director at Music Theater Wichita
where he has done many productions including Smokey Joe’s Café, The
Little Mermaid, Ragtime, Hairspray, The Full Monty, Miss Saigon, Beauty
and the Beast, and Les Misérables, among many others.
He has also conducted the Anchorage Opera, San Diego Symphony,
Seattle Symphony, Des Moines Symphony, Pittsburgh Symphony,
Pittsburgh Festival Opera, Canton Symphony, Wichita Symphony, Dallas
Symphony and the River City Brass Band. In addition, Thomas has
conducted classical guitarist and Latin Grammy winner Berta Rojas,
Tony winner Kelli O’Hara, and Leslie Odom Jr., Tony winner for his role
in Hamilton.
Thomas has enjoyed the stage roles of Amonasro in Elton John’s Aida,
Caiaphas in Jesus Christ Superstar and Balthazar in Amahl and the
Night Visitors. He has been a vocal soloist with the River City Brass
Band, Pittsburgh Opera, Pittsburgh Symphony, Canton Symphony, and
the Children’s Festival Chorus. He sang frequently with jazz legend Joe
Negri in his Mass of Hope. Thomas is the retired Professor Emeritus
Director of Opera Studies and Choral Activities at Carnegie Mellon
University. This is his 23rd season with the Bach Choir of Pittsburgh.

JEVON RUSHTON
Drums
Jevon Rushton is a Baltimore and
Pittsburgh-based drummer, teaching
artist, music director, and creative whose
work lives at the intersection of rhythm,
storytelling, education, and community. With
more than seven years as a teaching artist

the same spirit of musicality and collaboration into worship,
cultivating spaces where musicians serve not only the music,
but the moment. His artistry is rooted in connection: honoring
tradition, embracing innovation, and using music as a vessel to
awaken something human in every person it reaches.

PROGRAM NOTE
The Creation: When Man Plays God
by Bach Choir Member Elizabeth Arkush with contributions from Michael Friedrich
In celebration of Thomas Douglas’s final season with the Bach Choir, we
open with The Creation: When Man Plays God, a mashup of the classic
1931 masterpiece Frankenstein, directed by James Whale and starring
Boris Karloff in an iconic performance as Frankenstein’s monster, with a
funk-inspired “soundtrack” performed by the Bach Choir of Pittsburgh
and a 5-piece instrumental combo. Classical pieces like Purcell’s mournful
Dido’s Lament and the meditative Dies Irae (Ben Opie) are intermixed
with explosive funk jams and soul ballads, ranging from 70s favorites
like Give Up the Funk (Parliament) and Have You Seen Her (The Chi-
Lites) to grooves by modern funkocrats like Thundercat. Synced to the
movie scenes, these pieces echo the emotional rollercoaster of the film,
amplifying its pathos and terror, and highlighting its unintended ironies for a
modern-day audience.
Initially slated for the spring 2020 concert, Frankenstein Reimagined was
first performed by the Bach Choir in 2022. In a joyous season returning to
live performance after the pandemic, the choir – along with an outstanding
instrumental ensemble - showed they meant it when they sang, “We’re
gonna tear the roof off!” Now back once more in 2026, Frankenstein
Reimagined serves to celebrate the innovative vision of Artistic Director
Thomas Douglas in his last season with the Bach Choir.
Douglas’s musical choices are laced with humor and empathy. The
thunderous drum jam of “Frankenstein” (Edgar Winter Group) menaces the
seemingly sweet encounter of the creature with an innocent little girl. The
frenzied hunt of townsfolk and hounds for the monster is lightened with
Parliament’s classic anthem, “Give Up The Funk.” As brought to life by the
Bach Choir – and by Karloff’s unforgettable silver-screen performance –
pathos, romance, absurdity, and menace ring their changes over the film’s
brief 70 minutes.
In its conception, Frankenstein Reimagined recognizes both the danger and
hope of the future as artists of the past have portrayed it. Funk artists from
Parliament to Thundercat embrace futuristic motifs such as spaceships,
layering an Afrofuturist twist onto the worlds imagined by sci-fi. They make
an inspired pairing with Mary Shelley’s 1831 novel, which envisions future
technology and the new forces- and forms of oppression - it might give
birth to. Watching Frankenstein (1931) in 2026, the irony is inescapable: we
are at a moment when tech titans warn they may be on the brink of creating
a monster they can’t control. By the end, the film reminds us exactly who is
the monster in this story.
So what better way for us to celebrate the purely human than this rollicking,
soul-filled take on Frankenstein?

Musical & Narrative Highlights
Part I: Creation & Hubris
• Opening Groove & Title Sequence: Opening Groove -Paul Thompson/ Opening
Credits -Ben Opie
A dark instrumental groove sets a suspenseful tone, transitioning into new adapted lyrics
over the opening titles.
• The Graveyard & Desecration: Dido’s Lament (Henry Purcell)
As Henry Frankenstein and Fritz covertly unearth a freshly buried coffin and cut down a
hanged criminal, Purcell’s mournful ostinato—infused with a modern groove—emphasizes
the depth of their dark obsession.
• The Lecture & The Theft: Augustine (Blood Orange)
Inside the medical college, Dr. Waldman lectures on the physical differences between a
normal and abnormal brain. Fritz breaks in shortly after to steal a specimen, accompanied
by the contemporary R&B pulse of Blood Orange.
• Domestic Concerns: I Want to be Free (Ohio Players)
Elizabeth shares her worries about Henry’s isolation, while light funk rhythms and
instrumental solos riff on the theme of romance, concealing Henry’s obsession.
Part II: The Spark of Life
• The Laboratory Awakening: Dies Irae (arr. Ben Opie) and Them Changes
(Thundercat)
A violent storm gathers around the castle laboratory as Dies Irae sounds the ancient
warning of divine wrath. The driving beat of “Them Changes” pulses as lightning strikes
the apparatus, raising the creature and culminating in Henry’s famous cry, “It’s alive!”
• Premonitions & Wedding Plans: The List (Moonchild)
A propulsive chorus-and-band arrangement accompanies Elizabeth and Victor as they
warn Baron Frankenstein of Henry’s deteriorating sanity, while the Burgomaster arrives to
plan the wedding.
Part III: The Monster Unbound
• Innocence & Abuse: Bird’s Lament (Moondog) and A Brand New Me (Isaac Hayes)
Henry and Dr. Waldman debate the ethics of their experiment. The newborn creature,
framed by “A Brand New Me,” reaches toward the sun in wonder, only to be cowed by
Fritz’s torch and chained in darkness.
• Chaos & Struggle: Dangerous (Jaron Crawford, Thomas Douglas)
Chained and brutalized, the creature lashes out, killing Fritz and overpowering the
doctors. Complex, angular rhythms mirror the chaotic violence in the
laboratory dungeons.

• The Escape & The Return: Jameel’s Space Ride (Thundercat)
The Monster revives, strangles Dr. Waldman, and breaks free into the countryside,
celebrated with the zany futurism of “Jameel’s Space Ride.” As Henry returns home
to Elizabeth, keyboard church bells chime over a smooth bass riff.
Part IV: Tragedy & The Search
• Encounter at the Lake: Frankenstein (Edgar Winter Group)
Accompanied by the iconic rock instrumental, the creature wanders through the
forest and meets Little Maria, where their innocent interaction ends in a tragic
misunderstanding.
• Terror at the Manor: Have You Seen Her (The Chi-Lites)
While wedding guests celebrate downstairs, Elizabeth paces to a soulful rendition of
“Have You Seen Her,” sensing the impending danger of the monster’s break-in.
• The Discovery
The festivities are shattered as Maria’s grieving father carries her body through the
crowd, turning it toward vengeance.
Part V: The Chase & Finale
• The Search Party & The Windmill: Give Up The Funk (Parliament)
An explosive funk groove propels the torch-lit search through the mountains. The
Monster ambushes Henry and drags him to an abandoned windmill.
• The Inferno & Resolution: Frankenstein Reprise
Enraged with fear, the Monster hurls Henry from the tower as townsfolk set the
structure ablaze, trapping the creature under heavy beams. A gentle keyboard motif
brings the story to a close as Henry revives at home, concluding with a reprise of the
opening credits.
