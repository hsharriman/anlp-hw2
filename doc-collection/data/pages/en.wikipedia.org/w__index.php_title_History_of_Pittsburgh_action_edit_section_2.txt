==Early colonization (1747–1763)== [[File:French British Forts 1753 1758.png|thumb|right|270px|British and French Forts, 1753–1758, and the routes of the two British campaigns to take the forks of the Ohio]] [[File:Forts at Forks of Ohio.png|right|280px]] The first Europeans arrived in the 1710s as traders. Michael Bezallion was the first to describe the forks of the Ohio in a manuscript in 1717, and later that year European traders established posts and settlements in the area.<ref>{{cite web |title=Chronology |url=http://exhibit.library.pitt.edu/chronology/ |website=Historic Pittsburgh |publisher=University of Pittsburgh |access-date=19 October 2022}}</ref> Europeans first began to settle in the region in 1748, when the first [[Ohio Company]], a [[Colony of Virginia|Virginian]] land speculation company, won a grant of 200,000&nbsp;acres (800&nbsp;km<sup>2</sup>) in the upper Ohio Valley. From a post at present-day [[Cumberland, Maryland]], the company began to construct an {{convert|80|mi|km|adj=on}} [[wagon train|wagon road]] to the [[Monongahela River]]<ref name="Lorant">{{cite book |title=''Pittsburgh, The Story of an American City'', 5th edition |first=Stefan |last=Lorant |publisher=Derrydale Press |year=1999 |isbn=0-9674103-0-4 }}</ref> employing a Lenape Indian chief named [[Nemacolin]] and a party of settlers headed by Capt. [[Michael Cresap]] to begin widening the track into a road. It mostly followed the same route as an existing Native American trail<ref name="FortsCom"> {{cite book |url=https://archive.org/details/reportcommissio12unkngoog |title=The frontier forts of western Pennsylvania |author=Commission members: Thomas Lynch Montgomery, Henry Melchior Muhlenberg Richards, John M. Buckalew, George Dallas Albert, Sheldon Reynolds, Jay Gilfillan Weiser; compiled by George Dallas Albert |publisher=W.S. Ray, state printer|access-date=November 29, 2010 |page=[https://archive.org/details/reportcommissio12unkngoog/page/n450 382] |quote=Note: pp. 382 specifically discusses the 'Hanger' fort (literally in French: "storehouse") (a blockhouse) site on Redstone creek founded in 1754 on the ford; the Dunlap Creek site of [[Fort Burd]] is located on the bigger (canoe friendly) stream. |location=Report By the Commission to Locate the Site of the Frontier Forts of Pennsylvania |year=1916 }} </ref> now known as [[Nemacolin's Trail]]. The [[Ford (river)|river crossing]] and flats at [[Redstone Creek (Pennsylvania)|Redstone creek]], was the [[Redstone Old Fort|earliest point]] and shortest distance for the descent of a wagon road. Later in the war, the site fortified as [[Fort Burd]] (now [[Brownsville, Pennsylvania|Brownsville]]) was one of several possible destinations. Another alternative was the divergent route that became [[Braddock's Road]] a few years later through present-day [[New Stanton, Pennsylvania|New Stanton]]. In the event, the colonists did not succeed in turning the path into a wagon road much beyond the [[Cumberland Narrows]] [[mountain pass|pass]] before they came into conflict with Native Americans. The colonists later mounted a series of expeditions in order to accomplish piecemeal improvements to the track. The nearby Native American community of [[Logstown]] was an important trade and council center in the Ohio Valley.<ref name="Logs"/> Between June 15 and November 10, 1749, an expedition headed by [[Celeron de Bienville]], a French officer, traveled down the Allegheny and Ohio to bolster the French claim to the region.<ref name="FortsCom"/> De Bienville warned away British traders and posted markers claiming the territory.<ref>Crumrine, Boyd, L.H. Everts and Co. ''History of Washington County, Pennsylvania'', 1882, pg. 26.</ref> In 1753, [[Marquis Duquesne]], the [[Governor of New France]], sent another, larger expedition. At present-day [[Erie, Pennsylvania]], an advance party built [[Fort Presque Isle]]. They also cut a road through the woods and built [[Fort Le Boeuf]] on [[French Creek (Allegheny River)|French Creek]], from which it was possible at high water to float to the Allegheny. By summer, an expedition of 1,500 French and Native American men descended the Allegheny. Some wintered at the confluence of French Creek and the Allegheny. The following year, they built [[Fort Machault]] at that site.<ref name="Albert">{{cite book |title=The Frontier Forts of Western Pennsylvania |last=Albert |first=George |publisher=C. M. Busch |year=1896}}</ref> Alarmed at these French incursions in the Ohio Valley, [[Governor Dinwiddie]] of Virginia sent Major [[George Washington]] to warn the French to withdraw.<ref name="ftpitt"/> Accompanied by [[Christopher Gist]], Washington arrived at the Forks of the Ohio on November 25, 1753. [[File:George Washington's map, accompanying his "journal to the Ohio", 1754. LOC 99446116.jpg|thumb|left|290px|[[George Washington]]'s sketch of Monongahela River, Allegheny River (named as the upper Ohio), and French Creek that accompanied his journal (1753) in which he recorded his impressions:<ref name="Washington">{{cite book |title=The Diaries of George Washington, Vol. 1 |first=Donald |last=Jackson |editor-first=Dorothy |editor-last=Twohig |publisher=University Press of Virginia |year=1976}}</ref> {{cquote|As I got down before the Canoe, I spent some Time in viewing the Rivers, & the Land in the Fork, which I think extremely well situated for a Fort; as it has the absolute Command of both Rivers. The Land at the Point is 20 or {{convert|25|ft|m}} above the common Surface of the Water; & a considerable Bottom of flat well timber'd Land all around it, very convenient for Building.}}]] Proceeding up the Allegheny, Washington presented Dinwiddie's letter to the French commanders first at Venango, and then [[Fort Le Boeuf]]. The French officers received Washington with wine and courtesy, but did not withdraw.<ref name="ftpitt"/> Governor Dinwiddie sent Captain [[William Trent]] to build a [[fort]] at the Forks of the Ohio. On February 17, 1754, Trent began construction of the fort, the first European habitation<ref>{{cite book |title=Standard History of Pittsburg, Pennsylvania |editor-first1=Erasmus |editor-last1=Wilson |editor2=H.R. Cornell |display-editors=etal |year=1898 |page=58}}</ref> at the site of present-day [[Pittsburgh]]. The fort, named [[Fort Prince George]], was only half-built by April 1754, when over 500 French forces arrived and ordered the 40-some colonials back to Virginia. The French tore down the British fortification and constructed [[Fort Duquesne]].<ref name="Albert"/><ref name="ftpitt"/> Governor Dinwiddie launched another expedition. Colonel Joshua Fry commanded the regiment with his second-in-command, George Washington, leading an advance column. On May 28, 1754, Washington's unit clashed with the French in the [[Battle of Jumonville Glen]], during which 13 French soldiers were killed and 21 were taken prisoner.<ref>{{cite web| title=History and Culture|work=Fort Necessity National Battlefield|url = https://www.nps.gov/fone/learn/historyculture/index.htm| access-date=April 17, 2007}}</ref> After the battle, Washington's ally, Seneca chief [[Tanaghrisson]], unexpectedly executed the French commanding officer, Ensign [[Joseph Coulon de Jumonville]]. The French pursued Washington and on July 3, 1754, George Washington surrendered following the [[Battle of Fort Necessity]]. These frontier actions contributed to the start of the [[French and Indian War]] (1754–1763), or, the [[Seven Years' War]], a global confrontation between Britain and France fought in both hemispheres.<ref name="ftpitt"/><ref name="Anderson">{{cite book |title=Crucible of War: The Seven Years' War and the Fate of Empire in British North America, 1754–1766 |last=Anderson |first=Fred |publisher=Vintage Books |year=2000 |isbn=0-375-70636-4 |url-access=registration |url=https://archive.org/details/crucibleofwarsev00ande_0 }}</ref> [[File:Bouquet blockhouse.JPG|thumb|270px|The [[Fort Pitt Blockhouse]], dating to 1764, is the oldest structure in Pittsburgh.<ref name="ftpitt">{{cite web|title=A History of the Point |work=Fort Pitt Museum |url=http://www.fortpittmuseum.com/History.html |access-date=April 14, 2007 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070207152801/http://www.fortpittmuseum.com/History.html |archive-date=February 7, 2007 }}</ref>]] In 1755, the [[Braddock Expedition]] was launched, accompanied by [[Virginia militia]] officer [[George Washington]]. Two regiments marched from [[Fort Cumberland (Maryland)|Fort Cumberland]] across the [[Allegheny Mountains]] and into western Pennsylvania. Following a path Washington surveyed, over 3,000 men built a wagon road {{convert|12|ft|m}} wide, that when complete, was the first road to cross the [[Appalachian Mountains]]. [[Braddock Road (Braddock expedition)|Braddock's Road]], as it was known, blazed the way for the future [[National Road]] (US40). The expedition crossed the [[Monongahela River]] on July 9, 1755. French troops from Fort Duquesne ambushed Braddock's expedition at [[Braddock's Field]], nine miles (14&nbsp;km) from Fort Duquesne.<ref name="jubilee">''The Unwritten History of Braddock's Field (Pennsylvania)'', editor Geo. H. Lamb, A. M., Nicholson Printing Co., 1917</ref> In the [[Braddock expedition#Battle of the Monongahela|Battle of the Monongahela]], the French inflicted heavy losses on the British, and Braddock was mortally wounded.<ref name="EB1911"/> The surviving British and colonial forces retreated. This left the French and their Native American allies with dominion over the upper Ohio valley.<ref name="ftpitt"/> On September 8, 1756, an [[Kittanning Expedition|expedition of 300 militiamen]] destroyed the Shawnee and Lenape village of [[Kittanning (village)|Kittanning]], and in the summer of 1758, [[British Army]] officer [[John Forbes (General)|John Forbes]] began a campaign to capture Fort Duquesne.<ref name="EB1911"/> At the head of 7,000 regular and colonial troops, Forbes built [[Fort Ligonier]] and [[Fort Bedford]], from where he cut a wagon road over the Allegheny Mountains, later known as Forbes' Road. On the night of September 13–14, 1758, an advance column under [[James Grant (British Army officer, born 1720)|Major James Grant]] was annihilated in the [[Battle of Fort Duquesne]].<ref name="EB1911"/> The battleground, the high hill east of the Point, was named Grant's Hill in his memory. With this defeat, Forbes decided to wait until spring. But when he heard that the French had lost [[Fort Frontenac]] and largely evacuated [[Fort Duquesne]], he planned an immediate attack. Hopelessly outnumbered, the French abandoned and razed Fort Duquesne. Forbes occupied the burned fort on November 25, 1758, and ordered the construction of [[Fort Pitt (Pennsylvania)|Fort Pitt]], named after British Secretary of State [[William Pitt the Elder]]. He also named the settlement between the rivers, "Pittsborough" (see [[Etymology of Pittsburgh]]).<ref name="Lorant"/><ref name="ftpitt"/> The British garrison at Fort Pitt made substantial improvements to its fortification.<ref name="Lorant"/> The French never attacked Fort Pitt and the war soon ended with the [[Treaty of Paris (1763)|Treaty of Paris]] and French defeat.<ref name="ftpitt"/> They ceded their territories east of the Mississippi River.
