Begotten: Tuesday, November 3, 2026, 7:00 PM, New 4K Restoration!, 809 Liberty Ave Pittsburgh PA 15222
Organized by Pittsburgh Cultural Trust. Repeats: Runs Nov 3 – Nov 13 (11 days).
New 4K Restoration! Begotten has no characters in the ordinary sense. Its final credits name three principal figures: God Killing Himself. Mother Earth. Son of Earth — Flesh on Bone The entire film is what those names do to each other and to the earth. God Killing Himself tears open his body and dies. Mother Earth rises from what remains and takes his seed into herself. She gives birth to Son of Earth — Flesh on Bone. He is cast into a barren world, seized by the Nomads, tortured, killed, and briefly returned to life by his mother. The Nomads destroy her too. The Robed Figures gather what remains of mother and son, grind their bodies down, and return them to the soil. The ground flowers. Begotten was shot in black and white on 16mm reversal film, then rephotographed frame by frame through neutral-density filters. The process took four years and roughly ten hours of work for every finished minute. The image was worked by hand through repeated exposure and filtration until the grain itself began to move like an atmospheric condition, thickening the dark and tearing open the light. When the film first began circulating, no one agreed on where it belonged. Drama. Horror. Foreign. In one San Francisco video store, briefly, pornography. Susan Sontag encountered the film through the festival circuit, became one of its earliest and most important advocates and began showing it to people in her living room. For more than thirty-five years, Begotten circulated in versions that carried its force but not its complete photographic body. Now, for the first time, the sketch has become the painting. Nothing has been cleaned up or modernized. The image has simply arrived at the form it was always meant to possess: blacks with weight, whites with force, and grain breathing as atmosphere. Nobody has settled on what to call Begotten since it first appeared. That was never the problem. It is the reason to see it now. – E. Elias Merhige, July 2026 Directed by E. Chronovisor: Tuesday, November 3, 2026, 7:30 PM, Pittsburgh Cultural Trust, 809 Liberty Ave Pittsburgh, PA 15222
Organized by Pittsburgh Cultural Trust. Repeats: Runs Nov 3 – Nov 14 (12 days).
Benedictine monk Father Pellegrino Ernetti claimed to have invented, alongside Enrico Fermi and Wernher von Braun, a device called the Chronovisor, capable of transmitting events of the past as if live on TV. He professed to have witnessed orations by Cicero and the Crucifixion, though the evidence he provided, reported in the Italian press, was widely disputed. Taking this as their jumping-off point, filmmakers Kevin Walker and Jack Auen engage in a form of time travel as well, following Columbia scholar Béatrice Courte (real-life professor Anne-Laure Sellier), who comes across the Chronovisor while researching an unrelated topic and then, like countless hyperlink- or microfiche-surfing grad students before her, gets lost on an academic side quest. Traveling deeper and deeper into a stubbornly analog archive, she unearths an elaborate web of conjecture and conspiracy reaching all the way up to the highest echelons of the Vatican. Dense with on-screen text from real primary sources, scored to music by Gustav Holst, and shot on 16mm in the pooling shadows of many of New York City’s historic libraries, Chronovisor is a witty literary mystery about one of the many secrets that still hide out in libraries, waiting for someone with time, curiosity, and a JSTOR login to come along and disturb the dusty stacks - one of the year’s most singular and formally assured feature debuts. Produced by Washington and released on Brainfeeder, the album draws on Moroccan Gnawa, Arabic musical tradition, gospel, soul, and spiritual jazz, inspired in part by Kahlil Gibran's writing on healing and ancestral memory.
