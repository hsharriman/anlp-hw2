AI-SDM Student Brainstorming Session: Thursday, October 1, 2026, 12:00 PM - 1:00 PM, 1327 Wean Hall
Hosted by AI-SDM. Type: Career & Professional Development, Student Activities & Organizations.
Topic: Peer review at major research journals now involves hundreds of submissions per month, and the case for automated reviewer assignment is clear. What is less clear is how to do it well: unlike conference assignment, which can be solved offline once all submissions are in, journal assignment must be done online: each paper assigned immediately upon arrival, from a pool of reviewers who may become unavailable for weeks once assigned. We study this as an instance of online task assignment with reusable resources: tasks arrive over time, each must be immediately assigned to $k$ of $R$ available resources, and each assigned resource is busy for $d$ subsequent timesteps. This model captures peer review, healthcare scheduling, ridesharing, and emergency dispatch. I will present restless-bandit based policies and upper bounds. I will also discuss a closed-form Whittle index policy for automated reviewer assignment and insights from evaluation on a four-year trace of submissions to TMLR. Led by students of all levels, these sessions offer a casual forum to discuss AI-related research while exploring cross-cutting connections between various disciplines in artificial intelligence. ACO Seminar: Alexander Polyanskii (Emory University): Thursday, October 1, 2026, 3:00 PM - 4:00 PM, Wean Hall 8220
Hosted by Mathematical Sciences Department. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures, Open to the Public.
Speaker: Alexander Polyanskii , Emory University Tight colorful no-dimensional Tverberg theorem Abstract: In this talk, I will discuss a tight colorful dimension-free Tverberg theorem. If P1,…,Pk are finite disjoint sets of n points in a Euclidean space, then our result guarantees a partition of the union P1∪⋯∪Pk into n rainbow sets of size k such that a single ball intersects the convex hull of every set. The radius of this ball depends only on the number of color classes, the number of rainbow sets, and the largest diameter of a color class — not on the dimension of the ambient space. Moreover, the resulting bound is best possible. The key tool is a Pythagorean-type subadditivity property of the Chebyshev radius, which is of independent interest. Given sequences X=(x1,…,xn) and Y=(y1,…,yn) of points in a Euclidean space, contained in balls of radii RX and RY, respectively, one can re-enumerate Y so that the pointwise-sum sequence Z=X+Y is contained in a ball of radius satisfying R2Z≤R2X+R2Y. This is joint work with Polina Barabanshchikova and Grigory Ivanov.

Distinguished Speaker Series: Roozbeh Kiani: Thursday, October 1, 2026, 3:30 PM, Mellon Institute (MCS) 328
Hosted by Neuroscience Institute.
The Neuroscience Institute is honored to host distinguished lecturers on research topics that include cognitive, systems, or computational neuroscience or neuro-tech and engineering.
Dr. Roozbeh Kiani Professor, Neural Science and Psychology, New York University Thursday, October 1, 2026 3:30 PM Mellon Institute: Room 328 Summary: Flexible behavior requires flexible decision-making. We adapt seamlessly to changing environments—adjusting biases, altering decision rules, and inferring hidden task contexts—often without explicit cues. In this talk, I will outline a framework that formalizes different levels of this flexibility and show how these adjustments are implemented in neural codes across the frontoparietal cortex. I will highlight three forms of decision flexibility: (1) Bias adjustments, driven by asymmetric rewards, shift neural activity along the decision variable axis; (2) Rule changes, such as varying sensory weights in a multi-feature discrimination task, produce rotational changes in the population geometry, supporting rapid changes in decision policy; and (3) Hierarchical inference, where animals infer hidden contexts to adapt to task structure, is reflected in the emergence of latent variables represented in distributed subspaces.

Pitt/CMU AstroLunch: Emily Leiner (Illinois Institute of Technology): Friday, October 2, 2026, 11:00 AM, 3941 O’Hara Street, Pittsburgh, PA - Allen Hall - 321
Hosted by Physics Department. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures, Training & Workshops.
The Rotational Fingerprints of Post-Mass-Transfer Binaries
Speaker: Emily Leiner (Illinois Institute of Technology) Title: The Rotational Fingerprints of Post-Mass-Transfer Binaries Abstract: Mass transfer in binary systems creates a variety of important astronomical objects, including Type Ia supernovae and other transients now being discovered in time domain surveys. Yet many fundamental parameters governing mass transfer physics are still not well understood, including the criteria for mass transfer stability, angular momentum evolution, and the efficiency of mass transfer. In this talk I will focus on rotation as a diagnostic of recent mass transfer. I will discuss the rotational evolution of accreting stars, and demonstrate some new methods to detect post-mass-transfer populations that utilize the wealth of rotational data coming from TESS and other all-sky time domain surveys. I will further discuss a new approach to measuring masses and ages of post-mass-transfer stars in open clusters using the Bayesian code BASE-9. Combining these mass and age constraints with rotational information can yield critical new constraints on mass transfer stability and efficiency. Together, these results show that rotation is a largely untapped tool for discovering and characterizing a broad population of post-interaction binaries. Speaker: Florian Schaub Associate Professor of Information, School of Information and Associate Professor of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science, College of Engineering University of Michigan Talk Title: The Policy–Practice Gap in Privacy: Why Legal Protections Fall Short and How to Make Them More Effective Abstract: Around the world, data protection and privacy laws are proliferating as policymakers seek to protect consumers from harms arising in a data ecosystem fueled by the extensive collection, use, and sale of personal information. Yet these laws often fail to produce meaningful improvements in consumers’ privacy in practice. Dr. Schaub’s research investigates how public policy and legal requirements affect consumers’ privacy in practice. His empirical findings show that privacy notices rarely provide meaningful transparency into companies’ data practices; cookie banners do not support informed consent; despite the availability of privacy rights and controls, consumers often permit more data collection and third-party sharing than they expect; and effective identity-theft protections, such as credit freezes, continue to have low adoption rates. In this talk, Dr. Schaub will discuss his group’s empirical research on these issues and identify factors that create and sustain the privacy policy–practice gap. He will also discuss how human-centered privacy design and research-informed policymaking can help turn formal rights and requirements into effective protections for consumers. CMU/Pitt Colloquium: Natalia Perkins (University of Minnesota): Monday, October 5, 2026, 3:30 PM, Wean Hall, 7316
Hosted by Physics Department. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures, Training & Workshops.
Probing Fractionalized Excitations in Kitaev Spin Liquids.
Speaker: Natalia Perkins (University of Minnesota) Title: Probing Fractionalized Excitations in Kitaev Spin Liquids. Abstract: Quantum spin liquids are highly entangled states of matter in which conventional magnetic order is avoided even at very low temperatures. A particularly important example is the Kitaev spin liquid, which provides a rare exactly solvable setting where interactions between localized spins give rise to emergent fractionalized excitations. At the same time, Kitaev physics is believed to be relevant to a growing family of candidate quantum materials, making it possible to search experimentally for signatures of these exotic excitations. Establishing such signatures in real materials, however, remains a major challenge. In this colloquium, I will discuss several ways in which the fractionalized excitations of Kitaev spin liquids can be probed experimentally. I will focus in particular on lattice vibrations, which provide a natural window into the magnetic sector through spin-phonon coupling, and discuss how signatures of fractionalization can appear in phonon dynamics and Raman spectroscopy. I will also discuss local probes, including tunneling spectroscopy, and the perhaps counterintuitive role of disorder: vacancies and other defects need not simply obscure spin-liquid physics, but can generate characteristic local excitations and provide additional probes of the underlying fractionalized state. These examples illustrate a broader theme: rather than being passive complications, lattice vibrations and disorder can provide experimentally accessible fingerprints of fractionalization and help connect idealized quantum spin-liquid models to candidate materials. Fall Seminar - Andrej Ristesk: Monday, October 5, 2026, 4:00 PM, Posner Hall 151
Hosted by Statistics & Data Science Department. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures.
Inference-Time Algorithms: A Theoretical Lens on Tractability and Error Propagation
Abstract: Modern AI systems are increasingly built by placing trained models inside larger computational loops. Inference-time algorithms are a basic instance of this idea: they use one or more trained models at test time to incorporate new information, exploit pretrained models as priors, and trade computational effort for accuracy, sample quality, or control. Examples include generator-verifier search for reasoning, diffusion models for solving inverse problems, and reward-guided generation. Theoretically, this revisits a classical question from optimization and theoretical computer science: what can be done with access to an oracle? Here, however, the oracles are new and non-standard: they model the capabilities of large pretrained models, making them powerful, but also imperfect because they are learned. This combination leads to new questions about algorithm design and error propagation. This talk studies two central aspects of this paradigm: computational efficiency and error propagation. The first vignette considers generator-verifier systems, and shows how stochastic backtracking can trade additional computation for accuracy, giving a principled version of test-time scaling even with imperfect learned oracles. The second vignette studies diffusion steering: when can we efficiently bias a pretrained diffusion model toward higher-reward samples while staying close to the original model? We show that tractability depends strongly on both the reward structure and the alignment objective, and that simple primitives—such as sampling from linear tilts—can be surprisingly useful for handling richer reward classes. Based on https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03149 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16570 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11361 . Bio: Andrej Risteski is an Associate Professor at the Machine Learning Department in Carnegie Mellon University. Prior to that, he was a Norbert Wiener Research Fellow jointly in the Applied Math department and IDSS at MIT. He received his PhD in the Computer Science Department at Princeton University. His research interests lie in the intersection of machine learning, statistics, and theoretical computer science, spanning topics like generative models, algorithmic tools for learning and inference, representation and self-supervised learning, out-of-distribution generalization and applications of deep learning to scientific domains. The broad goal of his research is principled and mathematical understanding of statistical and algorithmic problems arising in modern machine learning paradigms.

Teaching Inclusively: Teaching in Tumultuous Times: Tuesday, October 6, 2026, 10:00 AM - 11:30 AM, Online
Hosted by Main Calendar. Type: Career & Professional Development, Training & Workshops.
A seminar for graduate students and postdocs hosted by the Eberly Center for Teaching Excellence & Educational Innovation
Instruction can be difficult for both teachers and students following a major incident or tragedy. These events may take an emotional and cognitive toll on students, disrupt their lives, and interfere with learning for extended periods of time. Students’ proximity to such an event does not always determine their response. For example, students may be seriously affected by incidents that involve total strangers. Additionally, students’ surface responses may not be indicative of the actual effect. As an instructor, it’s important to consider the impacts such events have on students as human beings and learners. In this seminar, we will discuss considerations for determining whether or not to address an event in the classroom and develop multiple strategies for addressing distressing events in your classes.

Condensed Matter Physics (CMP) Seminar: Michal Papaj (University of Houston): Tuesday, October 6, 2026, 12:00 PM, Wean Hall, 7316
Hosted by Physics Department. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures, Training & Workshops.
Pair density modulation in iron-based superconductors
Speaker: Michal Papaj (University of Houston) Title: Pair density modulation in iron-based superconductors Abstract: Pair density modulation (PDM) is a phenomenon recently observed in exfoliated flakes of iron-based superconductors [1], in which the superconducting gap oscillates strongly with the same periodicity as the underlying crystalline lattice. In this talk, I will present the current experimental evidence of PDM and discuss a model [2] that explains this modulation in systems with broken nonsymmorphic symmetries through the emergence of nematic superconductivity, which further breaks the four-fold rotation symmetry. This results in a sublattice texture on the Fermi surface, aligned with the anisotropic superconducting gap of the nematic s+d state. This gives rise to distinctive gap maxima and minima located on the two inequivalent iron sublattices while still being a zero-momentum pairing state. I will discuss how further investigation of such modulations can give insight into the nature of the superconducting pairing, such as the signs of the order parameters and visualization of a phase transition to a mixed two-component state using local probes. [1] L. Kong, MP, H. Kim, Y. Zhang, E. Baum, H. Li, K. Watanabe, T. Taniguchi, G. Gu, P. A. Lee, S. Nadj-Perge, Nature 640, 55-61 (2025) [2] MP, L. Kong, S. Nadj-Perge, P. A. Current Topics in Privacy Seminar: Sarah Radway: Tuesday, October 6, 2026, 12:30 PM - 2:00 PM, Hamburg Hall, Room 1002 and online
Hosted by Main Calendar. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures.
Priva-See: Privacy Leakage Through AI-Mediated Analysis of Smartphone Data
Over the past 30 years, the online advertising industry built a large-scale data collection ecosystem, with the goal of tracking a user’s online activity to infer their demographics and interests. Traditionally, the ecosystem relied upon the collation and analysis of highly-structured text data like user IP addresses, GPS coordinates, e-commerce purchase histories, and visited URLs. However, recent ML models can parse not only structured text, but also multimedia files and unstructured text inputs — meaning a user’s photos, videos, inboxes, and calendars are now ripe for automated analysis. The privacy risks are particularly acute in the context of smartphone apps. A user’s phone already acts as a natural collation point for sensitive user information, but users may not understand that permitting an app to, for example, access a user’s photo does not just give the app access to the bytes in the photo: the app also receives access to inferences about the user that are enabled by the photo. Priva-See, an LLM-based inference system for app-collected user data, was built to explore these privacy risks; Priva-See reflects our best understanding of how real-life adtech companies would leverage machine learning to build user profiles. Through an IRB-approved user study, 465 participants deployed Priva-See on their phones; Priva-See made privacy-invasive inferences despite having access to only a subset of a user’s data. We see the experience significantly impacted participant willingness to share permissions data moving forward. Based on the observed privacy violations, changes are suggested to how smartphone OSes should gather user consent for data access, to better inform users about downstream data usage capability. Sarah Radway is a computer science Ph.D. candidate at Harvard University, where she is advised by James Mickens. She is an NSF Graduate Research Fellow and an affiliate of the Berkman Klein Center. Her research involves building systems that are secure and private by design, as well as analyzing emerging technologies in ways that make privacy and security challenges understandable to policymakers and the public. She is particularly interested in privacy and security harms stemming from AI models, and is currently exploring systems-level safety interventions for frontier models. Geopolitics in the Age of AI - China and the Geopolitics of AI: Tuesday, October 6, 2026, 5:00 PM - 6:30 PM, Posner Hall, Grand Room (340)
Hosted by Carnegie Mellon Institute for Strategy & Technology. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures, Leadership & Civic Engagement.
Join CMIST for Geopolitics in the Age of AI with Jason Hsu, a senior fellow at Hudson Institute who focuses on the United States’ technological cooperation with allies and partners and Paul Triolo, Partner and Technology Policy Lead at DGA ASG, who advises clients in technology and financial services as they navigate complex political and regulatory matters.
Artificial Intelligence has become the central arena of US-China strategic competition, but the public debate — fixated on which country’s frontier model tops the latest benchmark — misses where the contest is actually being decided. This talk maps the layers that matter: compute and semiconductor supply chains, the export control regime and its mounting unintended consequences, and the industrial ecosystems that determine who deploys AI at scale. It examines how China’s AI sector has adapted under US restrictions — the surge of open-weight Chinese models in global adoption, Huawei’s advanced packaging workarounds, and Beijing’s countermoves on critical materials and state-directed capital. For policymakers, the uncomfortable finding is that several US controls are producing the opposite of their intended effects: accelerating Chinese substitution, eroding the market position of allied firms, and ceding influence over the global AI stack. The talk closes with the emerging third dimension of the relationship: AI safety and governance. In the wake of the first US-China dialogue on frontier model governance, it assesses whether Beijing’s rapidly maturing domestic safety-evaluation infrastructure represents a genuine basis for cooperation, a bid for standards-setting influence, or both — and what a realistic agenda for engagement looks like when the two countries are simultaneously racing and talking. Drawing on recent fieldwork in China, the talk offers a framework for judging which interventions actually work — and which are strategic self-harm dressed up as toughness. This dialogue will be moderated by Patrick Cronin, Carnegie Mellon University Scholar-in-Residence. Guest Speaker: Ankur Jain, PhD Associate Professor of Biology at MIT Core Member, Whitehead Institute Title: Keeping RNA in Shape: Folding, Misfolding, and Aggregation in Cells Our lab studies how RNA folds, misfolds, and aggregates in cells. RNA readily self-associates in a test tube, yet most cellular transcripts remain soluble. In this talk, I will discuss how this balance can break down in repeat-expansion disorders. We find that the sequences surrounding a repeat and aberrant splicing can alter where repeat-containing RNAs accumulate and lead to unexpected protein products, suggesting possible routes to neurological disease. I will then turn to the broader question of how cells limit aggregation across the transcriptome. Combining experiments and simulations, we find that native mRNA sequences are unusually resistant to self-association: folding within individual molecules helps limit intermolecular interactions and aggregation. These findings suggest that maintaining transcriptome solubility constrains genome evolution. Departmental Hosts: Professors McManus and Zhang

CERCA Virtual Guest Lecture with Dr. Xinyue Ye: Thursday, October 8, 2026, 11:00 AM - 12:00 PM, Zoom - Registration Required

Title : From Digital Twins to Autonomous Intelligence: GeoAI for ResilientCities and Infrastructure Abstract: Cities and infrastructure are complex spatial systems shaped by interactions among the built environment, human activities, mobility, and environmental processes. Drawing on a series of our studies using university campuses as living laboratories and microcosms of cities, this talk traces the evolution from GeoAI-enabled digital twins toward autonomous spatial intelligence. I will show how heterogeneous geospatial and human activity data can be integrated to represent physical and social systems, optimize spatial decisions, predict changing conditions and human behaviors, and incorporate spatial knowledge and constraints into AI reasoning. CMU/Pitt AstroLunch: Lina Necib (MIT): Friday, October 9, 2026, 11:00 AM, Wean Hall, 8330
Hosted by Physics Department. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures, Training & Workshops.
Dancing in the Dark: Data, Models, and the Milky Way’s Dark Matter
Speaker: Lina Necib (MIT) Title: Dancing in the Dark: Data, Models, and the Milky Way’s Dark Matter Abstract: In this talk, I will explore the interfacing of simulations, observations, and machine learning techniques to construct a detailed map of dark matter in the Milky Way, and argue that progress now hinges on the dance between the information content of our data and the fidelity of our models. For the Galactic halo, I will present recent work leveraging Gaia DR3 that reveals a declining stellar rotation curve. I will then show how underestimated errors can bias the resulting curve, and how we used simulations to build a more robust error treatment that reconciles the measurement with other probes of the Galaxy’s enclosed mass. In dwarf galaxies, stellar data of the faintest systems will not substantially grow and therefore I present GraphNPE, a graph neural network methodology that accurately extracts dark matter density profiles from sparse stellar kinematics. With GraphNPE, we find a hint of a core in Boötes I, consistent with predictions of self-interacting dark matter. Finally, I will invert the question and quantify, using mutual information, how the memory of the Milky Way’s merger history is progressively erased from stellar dynamics, setting a fundamental limit on what Galactic archaeology can recover. Together, these results outline a program of inference that seeks to extract the most robust model of dark matter in the Milky Way from available data, arriving just as Gaia DR4 and the next generation of surveys come online. Soft Materials Initiative Seminar: Friday, October 9, 2026, 3:00 PM, Scott Hall (6142)
Hosted by Materials Science and Engineering Department.
“WireOx: An Electrochemical Oxygen Generation Platform for Cell Survival and Cell Actuation” presented by Rita Aguyé Cots, MSE Ph.D. Student
Living pharmacy implants use engineered cells to synthesize and release therapeutic proteins on demand, offering closed-loop treatment of chronic diseases without repeated injections. Reaching clinically relevant doses requires dense cell encapsulation, but these cells consume oxygen faster than passive diffusion can supply it. Existing oxygen sources either deplete rapidly or lack release control. In this talk, we present WireOx, an implantable device that generates oxygen on demand by catalyzing water oxidation at sputtered iridium oxide films on platinum wire, with output precisely tuned by applied current. We will show that controlled oxygenation sustains cell viability in high-density grafts and outline our progress toward in vivo validation. Furthermore, we demonstrate that controlled oxygen levels can actuate cell output through an oxygen-responsive genetic circuit within a narrow window bounded by hypoxic and hyperoxic stress. Finally, we will discuss the implications of this therapeutic window for designing oxygen-managed living pharmacy implants.

Speaker: Regina Demina (University of Rochester) Title: Quantum observables at colliders Abstract: The behavior of elementary particles is governed by quantum field theory, yet demonstrating their quantum nature experimentally is far from straightforward. Until recently, such demonstrations were either performed at relatively low energies or involved only composite particles. Recent results from the LHC have challenged this status quo by demonstrating entanglement in a system of the heaviest known fundamental particles—the top quarks. A top-antitop quark pair produced at high energies constitutes a two-qubit system. The top quark has an extremely short lifetime of approximately 5 × 10−25 s, shorter than the characteristic hadronization timescale, 1/ΛQCD ≈ 10−24 s. Detailed studies of the kinematics in this region are consistent with the presence of such a bound-state contribution. In my talk, I will present measurements of top-antitop quark entanglement and discuss the investigation of possible toponium formation. Distinguished Speaker Series: Pawan Sinha: Thursday, October 15, 2026, 12:00 PM, Mellon Institute Room 348
Hosted by Neuroscience Institute.
Of the estimated one million blind children worldwide, over 80% live in low- and middle- income countries. For many, limited access to medical care results in years of unnecessary blindness, even when their condition is surgically treatable. Project Prakash was founded to address this humanitarian need while simultaneously pursuing a fundamental scientific question: How does the human brain learn to see? Through rural ophthalmic screening programs in India, Project Prakash identifies children with treatable blindness and provides them with high-quality sight-restoring surgery at no cost to their families. The onset of sight after years of congenital blindness creates a rare scientific opportunity - by following these children longitudinally, we can investigate how the brain learns to extract structure and meaning from visual information, whether and how different perceptual abilities emerge, and how the potential for neural plasticity changes across development. This talk will describe Project Prakash’s integration of medical service and scientific discovery and present findings from behavioral, electrophysiological, and neuroimaging studies of children gaining sight after prolonged blindness. These studies reveal both notable capacities for visual learning and enduring consequences of early deprivation, providing insights into the mechanisms and limits of human brain plasticity. More broadly, Project Prakash illustrates how addressing an urgent societal need can itself create opportunities for fundamental scientific discovery that, in time, may help improve interventions for children with sensory and developmental disorders.

Unraveling the nanoscale architecture and multifunctional roles of cytoskeletal scaffolds with super-resolution imaging
Speaker: Ruoho Zhou (Penn State University) Title: Unraveling the nanoscale architecture and multifunctional roles of cytoskeletal scaffolds with super-resolution imaging Abstract: Super-resolution fluorescence imaging has transformed our ability to visualize molecular organization at nanometer scales, yet imaging complex assemblies over extended timescales and across multiple protein species remains challenging. We recently developed FLEXTAG, a compact, self-renewable, anti-photobleaching protein-labeling platform enabling multiplexed and sustained super-resolution imaging. We applied these approaches to the neuronal membrane-associated periodic skeleton (MPS), a lattice-like cortical structure of actin, spectrin, and associated molecules that provides a powerful model for understanding how nanoscale organization shapes cellular function. Extending this framework beyond the nervous system, we are applying the same super-resolution and quantitative single-molecule approaches to ask whether cytoskeletal scaffolds play an analogous organizing role in disease, including in the constitutive signaling of oncogenic kinase fusions. Together, this work demonstrates the power of super-resolution imaging to move from resolving nanoscale structures to uncovering how molecular architecture governs cellular function in health and disease. Fall Seminar - Samuel Baugh: Monday, October 19, 2026, 4:00 PM, Posner Hall 151
Hosted by Statistics & Data Science Department. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures.
Stochastic Interventions for Causal Inference in Climate Science
Abstract: Understanding the extent to which increases in atmospheric aridity are driven by temperature versus precipitation changes is important for informing adaptation efforts for crop stress and wildfire risk. Because controlled experiments on the global climate system are impossible, this is an observational causal inference question, complicated by the high dimension of the treatment field. To answer causal inference questions in climate science, we propose a spatio-temporal stochastic intervention framework with a conditional shift estimand and a regularized inverse probability weighting estimator with a tunable penalty parameter for balancing the bias-variance tradeoff. We show through simulation studies that this estimator outperforms alternative IPW-based estimators at recovering the true causal effect, and correctly adjusts for the confounding bias incurred by an unadjusted estimator. Applying this framework to the estimation of the causal effect of temperature on aridity, adjusted for precipitation, in climate-model simulations, we show that the effect of historical warming on vapor-pressure deficit emerges in the mid-1990s, and that an unadjusted estimator inflates the global estimate by nearly half. How does online opposition to vaccination become a critique of established sources of knowledge and authority? AI-SDM Student Brainstorming Session: Thursday, October 22, 2026, 12:00 PM - 1:00 PM, 1327 Wean Hall
Hosted by AI-SDM. Type: Career & Professional Development, Student Activities & Organizations.
Led by: Keerthana Gurushankar Peer review at major research journals now involves hundreds of submissions per month, and the case for automated reviewer assignment is clear. What is less clear is how to do it well: unlike conference assignment, which can be solved offline once all submissions are in, journal assignment must be done online: each paper assigned immediately upon arrival, from a pool of reviewers who may become unavailable for weeks once assigned. We study this as an instance of online task assignment with reusable resources: tasks arrive over time, each must be immediately assigned to $k$ of $R$ available resources, and each assigned resource is busy for $d$ subsequent timesteps. This model captures peer review, healthcare scheduling, ridesharing, and emergency dispatch. I will present restless-bandit based policies and upper bounds. I will also discuss a closed-form Whittle index policy for automated reviewer assignment and insights from evaluation on a four-year trace of submissions to TMLR. Unveiling the “Fundamental Physics” Within and Around Binary Radio Pulsars
Speaker: Emmanuel Fonseca (WVU) Title: Unveiling the “Fundamental Physics” Within and Around Binary Radio Pulsars Abstract: The interplay of extreme gravity and nuclear astrophysics within neutron stars has led to an ongoing, century-long debate over the possible states of matter within their interiors. Multi-messenger measurements of neutron-star observables allow us to constrain their elusive “equation of state” (EoS), i.e., the statistical-mechanical relation that connects macroscopic parameters to the underlying microphysics. In this talk, I will describe how binary radio pulsars — rotating neutron stars that emit beamed radiation in orbit with other massive objects — will continue to provide influential measurements for probing aspects of “fundamental physics,” such as EoS physics and strong-field gravity. Graduate Student Seminar: Friday, October 23, 2026, 12:30 PM, Wean Hall (Room 7500)
Hosted by Materials Science and Engineering Department.
“Aeroplastic design for space structures,” presented by Natasha Vermaak (Lehigh University)
ABSTRACT: Turbine disks that are used in oxygen-rich turbopumps for reusable rocket engines are subjected to centrifugal forces from high-speed rotation and rapid cyclic thermal transients. These loading conditions are challenging to mitigate in order to maximize thermal performance for engine efficiency while avoiding burst, fatigue, and frictional ignition failures. Typically, yield-limited design rules are used to avoid thermal fatigue failures while still maximizing allowable thermal gradients in the disk. In this study, alternative design rules are considered that leverage the plastic reserve while still safeguarding against burst and frictional ignition. In particular, both finite life design strategies and design rules based on the underused elastoplastic shakedown phenomenon are explored. Dr. Natasha Vermaak is an Associate Professor of Mechanical Enginering and Mechanics at Lehigh University. She leads the Thermostructural Lab at the intersection of mechanics, materials, and design/optimization. Her group uses experimental and computational techniques to address problems of structural and material degradation, durability, and resilience. Her group applies optimization to design materials/structures at various scales. This includes the design and analysis of multifunctional architected materials and structures, especially in the inelastic and finite life domains. She has unique expertise in inelastic design via shakedown and seeks to harness cyclic plastic behaviors for metals in extreme environments that span aerospace (“aeroplasticity”) to energy applications. Dr. Vermaak earned her doctorate in Materials (2010) from the University of California Santa Barbara. Before joining Lehigh University in 2013 as an Assistant Professor, she served as a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Grenoble in France. She is the inaugural 2020 Frontiers of Materials Awardee of The Minerals, Metals & Materials Society, the Founding Chair of the Design of Engineering Materials (DEM) Technical Committee within the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME), and a 2026 Fulbright scholar.

Colloquium: Hannah Rubin, Ph.D.: Friday, October 23, 2026, 3:30 PM - 4:50 PM, Baker Hall A36
Hosted by Philosophy Department. Type: Conferences, Panels & Lectures, Open to the Public.
Citation Norms and Citation Gaps Beck Apicello and Hannah Rubin
Citation gaps – systematic undercitation of women and minoritized scholars – are both an ethical and epistemic concern for scientific communities. We argue that these gaps emerge from field‑level norms about how much to read, how much to cite, and whether to prioritize relevance or prestige. We develop an agent-based model to analyze how these factors interact with demographic representation and homophily to create citation gaps. We also introduce an evolutionary model to examine how citation gaps change as representation increases over time and show that whether gaps diminish depends on a field’s relationship to the past: communities that weight recent work more heavily tend to see gaps shrink, while those anchored in canonical literature may sustain them even as representation grows. The analysis clarifies how local norms and long‑term dynamics jointly structure citation inequality.

Seminar: Designing & Printing Dispersion Strengthened Ti-6Al-4V via Laser Powder Bed Fusion Additive Manufacturing: Monday, October 26, 2026, 1:00 PM - 2:00 PM, Virtual
Hosted by NextManufacturing.
This Next Manufacturing seminar features a talk by Dr. Amaranth Karra , Postdoctoral Researcher, University of North Texas.
Abstract The anisotropy of the mechanical properties of Ti-6Al-4V additively manufactured (AM) via laser powder bed fusion (PBF-LB) is increased by the long and textured columnar prior beta grains that typically occur despite the high cooling rates (105–107 K/s). Additionally, both AM and conventionally fabricated Ti-6Al-4V exhibit dwell-fatigue sensitivity at room-temperature, resulting in premature failure in the low-cycle fatigue regime. This work explores the design and printing of yttria dispersion-hardened Ti-6Al-4V with minimal powder feedstock preparation. A uniformly dispersed yttria with a mean particle size of∼100 nm in the Ti-6Al-4V matrix decreases the transverse size of the prior beta grains. Furthermore, STEM bright-field micrographs reveal dispersoid pinning of dislocations, and STEM EDS confirms that the dispersoids are yttria as expected. A modest increase in room-temperature microhardness is observed, and room-temperature nanoindentation creep results show a∼64% lower mean creep rate compared to standard PBF-LB Ti-6Al-4V. Additionally, a room-temperature compression test was performed to determine the respective yield strengths to study cold creep, and its implications are discussed. At normalized stresses, ODS Ti-6Al-4V exhibits substantially lower long-term creep rates, albeit with greater initial deformation. This work successfully demonstrates the use of PBF-LB to fabricate oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) Ti-6Al-4V, enabling refinement of the prior beta grains and improved long-term time-dependent deformation at normalized stresses. Bio Amaranth Karra is a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Mechanical Engineering at the University of North Texas. He received his Ph.D. and M.S. in Materials Science and Engineering from Carnegie Mellon University. His research focuses on process metallurgy, additive manufacturing, and process–structure–property relationships in advanced metallic materials. His work spans laser powder bed fusion and directed energy deposition of refractory alloys, titanium alloys, and oxide dispersion-strengthened materials, with an emphasis on understanding defect formation, phase evolution, and process optimization using advanced characterization techniques, including in situ synchrotron X-ray imaging and diffraction. His current research investigates the additive manufacturing of dispersion-strengthened Ti–6Al–4V alloys, while his doctoral research focused on additive manufacturing of tungsten and tungsten-based alloys for extreme-environment applications.

Pitt/CMU Colloquium: Christina Marchetti (UCSB): Monday, October 26, 2026, 3:30 PM, 3943 O’Hara Street, Pittsburgh, PA 15213, Thaw Hall 102

Active Matter: The Physics of Self-Organization
Active Matter: The Physics of Self-Organization Birds flock, bees swarm and fish school. These are just some of the remarkable examples of collective behavior found in nature. Physicists have been able to capture some of this behavior by modeling organisms as ``flying spins’’ that align with their neighbors according to simple but noisy rules. Successes like these have spawned a field devoted to the physics of active matter – matter made not of atom and molecules but of entities that consume energy to generate their own motion and forces. Through interactions, collectives of such active particles organize in emergent structures on scales much larger than that of the individuals. Living systems are the paradigm of such behavior, but same behavior has also been mimicked in the lab through the engineering a variety of ``active particles’’ that self-assemble to form smart materials. In this lecture I will introduce the field of active matter and highlight ongoing efforts by physicists, biologists, engineers and mathematicians to model the complex behavior of these systems, with the goal of identifying universal principles. I will specifically focus on two examples of active behavior. The first highlights how active particles bypass the laws of equilibrium thermodynamics and spontaneously aggregate in the absence of any attractive interactions. The second describes the interplay of flow and topological defects in controlling dynamics and structure of active phases with liquid crystalline order, with relevance from materials science to biology. CNA seminar: Amit Acharya (Carnegie Mellon University): Tuesday, October 27, 2026, 1:00 PM - 2:00 PM, Wean Hall 7218

An Adaptive Variational Dual Scheme for equation solving Abstract: I will describe a scheme for converting the question of solving a given system of equations and associated side-conditions into a variational problem whose formal Euler-Lagrange system recovers the given system and side-conditions using a change-of-variables generated by the scheme. The variational principle is convex, and the method uses a string of such convex variational principles to seek a solution of the original question as an interior minimizer of the last variational problem of the string. Weak coercivity of a variational problem in the string endows its minimizer the status of a weaker `variational dual solution’ to the original, possibly non-variational problem. This last point of view is directly related to Y. Brenier’s Hidden Convexity approach to nonlinear PDE. Due to the inherent convexity of the scheme, it allows for obtaining `unstable’ solutions of the original, primal system of equations in a `stable’ manner. A ChatGPT-enabled exploration of the scheme in the context of approximation of the mixed curvature isometric embedding problem from R^2 to R^3 will be discussed, as well as the problem of finding deformations of a membrane with prescribed principal stretch fields. This last problem is practically important for questions of sensing and actuation. The event will be available both by Zoom and in Wean Hall 7218.

Five Lessons on Caregiving from Wilderness Survival: Wednesday, October 28, 2026, 5:00 PM - 7:00 PM, Frank-Ratchye STUDIO for Creative Inquiry (CFA 111)
Hosted by Center for the Arts in Society. Type: Community Outreach, Conferences, Panels & Lectures, Open to the Public.
Join Virginia Eubanks as she shares her experience of family caregiving, trauma and “collateral” PTSD. Drawing on lessons from wilderness survival and conversations with experts and everyday heroes, she explores how we can build resilient communities of care in uncertain times.
In 2015, after a violent attack on her beloved partner, Virginia Eubanks suddenly became a family caregiver. In the weeks, months, and years that followed, she and her partner struggled to stay afloat as they faced wave upon wave of setbacks, including a serious case of post-traumatic stress disorder. Then, a second case. Eubanks developed “collateral” PTSD, a condition common among caregivers but rarely discussed. An old lifesaving manual provided unexpected help. Inspired by its lessons, Eubanks studied wilderness first aid, kayak self-rescue, winter survival, bushwhacking, and lifeguarding. She interviewed experts and everyday heroes—from neuroscientists to monks to forest rangers. Join Eubanks to discover what it takes to build communities of care that can sustain us in uncertain times.

Chemistry Seminar: Anindita Das, Southern Methodist University: Friday, October 30, 2026, 12:30 PM, Mellon Institute Conference Room 348
Hosted by Chemistry Department.
Tuning Atomically Precise Materials for Sustainable Chemistry
Anindita Das Southern Methodist University Tuning Atomically Precise Materials for Sustainable Chemistry Host: Stefan Bernhard Friday, October 30th, 2026 12:30 p.m. Mellon Institute Conference Room 348 Abstract: With the advent of nanotechnology in the mid-1980s, nanomaterials have attracted tremendous interest due to their unique properties which make them suitable for diverse applications. However, the inherent polydispersity of conventional nanoparticles has precluded an atomic-level understanding of their structure-property relationships. This, in turn, has greatly hampered the complete realization of their target functional properties in real-world applications. This talk will focus on leveraging the principles of synthetic organic and inorganic chemistry to engineer multifunctional atomically precise materials for applications in catalysis, water harvesting, and iodine capture. Specifically, we will discuss two classes of materials, (1) molecularly pure, ligand-protected coinage metal clusters, and (2) covalent organic frameworks (COFs), which are atomically precise tunable materials with extraordinary porosity and crystallinity. Overall, these efforts aim to establish design rules for making functional heterogeneous nanomaterials with known structure-property-function relationships. Bio: Anindita (Dia) Das obtained her Ph.D. with Prof. Rongchao Jin at Carnegie Mellon University, where her research focused on the synthesis and single-crystal growth of atomically precise gold nanoparticles to gain in-depth understanding of catalytic mechanisms. Prior to this, she earned B.Sc. and M.Sc. degrees at Osmania University, India and the University of Pune, India respectively. Following her PhD., Dia carried out postdoctoral research with Prof. Chad Mirkin at Northwestern University, where her work centered on the development of spherical nucleic acids based on atomically precise gold nanoclusters for applications in colloidal crystal engineering. Dia started her independent lab in the Chemistry Department at Southern Methodist University in Fall 2020.
