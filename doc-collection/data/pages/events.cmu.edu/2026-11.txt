Hosted by Libraries. Type: Arts & Performance, Campus Life, Open to the Public.
This exhibition inaugurates University Libraries’ newly renovated Posner Center, surveying its collections and tracing more than four centuries of scientific inquiry and learning.
The Posner Center is a cabinet of rarities and technological marvels: paper-and-string cipher machines, centuries-old books, cogwheel computers and 19th century “digital” images. Part museum and part laboratory, it is a site for discovery, research, and innovation with the materials of the past. Rare Books & Ancestral Machines features more than 30 books and objects from the Posner Center’s holdings. Many of these materials are on view for the first time. The exhibition inaugurates the newly renovated Center, surveying its collections and tracing more than four centuries of scientific inquiry and learning. Organized into five thematic sections — mechanical computing, robotics, cryptography, artificial intelligence, and scholarly method — the exhibit explores the histories of these disciplines, uncovering abandoned prototypes and long-forgotten technologies. What will you discover? Exhibit Hours During the summer, the Posner Center operates by appointment only . Contact Sam Lemley to make an appointment to visit the exhibit. Guest Lecture: Driving Measurable Value Through Owner Focused Innovation: Monday, November 2, 2026, 10:00 AM - 11:00 AM, Porter Hall A7F
Hosted by Civil and Environmental Engineering Department.
Title: Driving Measurable Value Through Owner Focused Innovation Abstract: Digital transformation in the built environment is increasingly defined not by technology adoption alone, but by the ability to demonstrate measurable value. AI, openBIM, VDC, digital twins, Lean practices, and performance metrics are becoming more closely connected as organizations seek better project, asset, and business outcomes. Drawing on more than 30 years of experience in openBIM and digital innovation, including owner-focused initiatives across 30 countries, Dr. Calvin Kam will explore how governments, owners, and industry organizations can translate emerging technologies, open standards, and mandates into practical implementation strategies and measurable results. Building on his work as Founder and CEO of Strategic Building Innovation, his work with buildingSMART International on Global openBIM Mandates and openBIM Business Value, and research associated with Stanford University’s Center for Integrated Facility Engineering (CIFE), the presentation will examine how frameworks such as the VDC Scorecard can help organizations connect information requirements, quality controls, technology adoption, KPIs, and performance with broader business value. Looking forward, the session will explore how AI, openBIM, and VDC can increasingly enable digital twins, GIS, IoT, sustainability, and decarbonization - creating a stronger foundation for informed decision-making, smarter mandates, and improved whole-lifecycle performance across the built environment. Bio: Dr. Calvin Kam is Founder & CEO of Strategic Building Innovation and Strategic Green Innovation — the “GPS Navigator” for any enterprise charting a course for construction innovation while optimizing the value of built assets. Dr. Kam serves as the Chapter President of buildingSMART Chapter in the United States and an Executive of buildingSMART International’s Compliance Program. He received his Doctor of Philosophy degree from Stanford University in Design/Construction Integration and Construction Engineering & Management. As an Adjunct Professor at Stanford University’s Center for Integrated Facility Engineering (CIFE) and a Co-Founder of GSA’s National BIM Program, Dr. Kam advises built asset owners globally on strategic innovation, including customized AI applications and digitalized construction management leveraging openBIM/GIS global standards. Dr. Kam has advised public agencies in APEC, Finland, Hong Kong China, Norway, UAE, U.K., U.S., and Singapore on AI, digitalization, innovation, open standards, productivity, value chain, and management science. Elevated as a Fellow of the American Institute of Architects as well as buildingSMART International, Dr. Kam has given keynotes and lectures in over 30 countries and has been published in industry handbooks, APEC official publications, journals, and conference papers. He has been recognized through ENR’s “20 Under 40” and BD+C’s “40 Under 40” awards, and honored with fellowships by ASCE, SOM, and Stanford University. Hosted by Professor Pingbo Tang

About the Artist Kearra Amaya Gopee (born 1994, Miami, FL, raised in Carapichaima, Kairi (Trinidad and Tobago); lives on Lenape land in New York) is an anti-disciplinary artist and facilitator. Graduate Student Seminar: Friday, November 6, 2026, 12:30 PM, Wean Hall (Room 7500)
Hosted by Materials Science and Engineering Department.
From Materials Sustainability to Quantum Devices: First-Principles Insights into Dynamic Interfaces, presented by Elizabeth Lee (UC Irvine)
Abstract: Material interfaces are critical frontiers where macroscopic functionality emerges from microscopic phenomena—where bonds break, charges migrate, and heat carriers scatter. While first-principles molecular dynamics (FPMD) provides atomic-level insight into these processes, high computational cost has traditionally restricted its application to small systems (hundreds of atoms) and short timescales (picoseconds). I will discuss our approach for overcoming these limitations by integrating FPMD with Bayesian Inference, neural networks, and rare-event sampling. By bridging the gap between quantum fidelity and long-time and length-scale phenomena, we explore three distinct technological frontiers: (i) reactive chemistry at polymer–metal interfaces for sustainable plastic recycling, (ii) nanoscale heat transfer in alloys in the presence of grain boundaries and dopants, and (iii) charge transfer at semiconductor–water interfaces to realize robust nanoscale quantum sensors. Overall, these results show how predictive, first-principles knowledge of interfacial behavior can provide a rational design roadmap for the next generation of chemical recycling, thermal management, and quantum technologies. Elizabeth “Liza” Lee is a Samueli Faculty Development Chair Assistant Professor with joint appointments in the Department of Materials Science and Engineering and the Department of Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering at the University of California, Irvine (UCI). She is also affiliated with the Advanced Casting Research Center (ACRC) and the Eddleman Quantum Institute. She leads the Computational Engineering of Material Interfaces (CeMi) Lab, which develops quantum-classical simulation frameworks—combining electronic structure methods, machine learning, and molecular modeling—to reveal atomic-scale mechanisms at dynamic material interfaces. Her research interests include heterogeneous catalysis, semiconductor defects, and thermal transport in alloys. Before joining UCI, she was a postdoctoral scholar in the Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering at the University of Chicago, where she developed first principles computational frameworks with neural networks to investigate chemical bonding dynamics and equilibria in quantum materials and at metal surfaces. She earned her Ph.D. from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), where she studied nanoscale energy transport phenomena in molecular semiconductors - such as colloidal quantum dots and conjugated polymers—to design next generation photovoltaics and LEDs. Her honors include the NSF CAREER Award, the UCI Samueli Faculty Development Chair Award, and the DOE ASCR Leadership Computing Challenge Award. She has also been recognized for excellence in teaching and mentoring, including receiving the UCI Engineering Student Council’s Professor of the Year Award.

EPP Seminar: Sonia Mangones — Transportation, Air Pollution, and Inequalities: Evidence from Bogotá, Colombia: Monday, November 9, 2026, 12:00 PM - 1:00 PM, Wean Hall 4110
Hosted by Engineering and Public Policy Department.
Environmental justice research often examines the burden of pollution in relation to the population’s socioeconomic vulnerability. Climate justice research examines differential responsibility, asking who emits and who ultimately carries the burden they create. Urban mobility justice research rarely combines these two questions empirically, even though mobility is where they intersect most directly, since travel generates emissions and also shapes where resulting pollution concentrations fall across the city. This talk presents findings from an empirical study of Bogotá, Colombia, that measures both sides of this relationship at the level of city blocks, combining residential pollution concentration (NO2 and PM2.5) with a block-level proxy for travel-emission responsibility built from official mobility and emissions data. Two findings challenge a simple story in which the poor pollute least and suffer most. First, pollution concentration does not increase monotonically with deprivation; it peaks at intermediate socioeconomic levels. Second, emission responsibility does decline cleanly with vulnerability, but the resulting imbalance, where responsibility is low and pollution concentration is high, concentrates in population segments that can only be identified by combining policy instruments that separately inform socioeconomic vulnerability and the spatial distribution of the imbalance. I discuss what this means for the mobility justice conversation and the implications for transportation and environmental policy. Speaker: Veit Elser , Cornell University A greedy method for solving even hard problems Abstract: Greed is a great instinct for solving problems. Its geometrical analog is a projection. Say your problem has many variables. It’s always possible to let them live in a continuous space, and define solutions to your problem as some subset in that space. A projection is the greedy operation of moving an arbitrary point to the set as efficiently as possible — by the least Euclidean distance. For many kinds of sets, even discrete and other highly non-convex ones, the projection computations are easy. Of course we know that cannot be the case when the task at hand is truly hard. Fortunately, even hard problems can be formulated as finding a point in the intersection of two sets, where projecting to each set individually is easy. This talk describes a general method that uses two easy projections, iteratively, to solve hard problems. After some history I will go over several examples, including sudoku, square packing, and data clustering. Problem requests will be considered if received (at ve10@cornell.edu) at least one week before the talk.

Graduate Student Seminar: Friday, November 20, 2026, 12:30 PM, Wean Hall (Room 7500)
Hosted by Materials Science and Engineering Department.
“Cooking, Fishing and Jogging through Phase Space: A Practical Guide to Discovering and Understanding New Materials,” presented by Paul C. Canfield (Iowa State University)
Abstract: The design, discovery, characterization and control of novel materials is perhaps the most important research area for humanity as it moves into the 21rst century. A myriad of societal problems concerning energy, clean water and air, and medicine all need to be solved by the discovery of new compounds with dramatically improved, or even new, properties. The search for such materials requires a blending of skills and mindsets that, traditionally, have been segregated into different academic disciplines: physics, chemistry, metallurgy, materials science. In this colloquium I will outline the basic philosophy and techniques that we use to search for novel materials. These include a combination of intuition, experience, compulsive optimism and a desire to share discovery.[1] In the second half of the lecture, the specific case of superconductivity will be used as an example of one such search. Over the past couple of decades a growing sense of where and even how to search for new superconductors has been developing, with the recent discoveries of MgB2 and the FeAs based materials providing, at least for me, clear guidance. [2] [1] Paul C. Canfield, Rep. Prog. Phys. 83 [2020] 016501. [2]Paul C. Canfield, Nature Materials 10 [2011] 259. Paul C. Canfield is an Ames Laboratory Senior Scientist, a Distinguished Professor and the Robert Allen Wright Professor of Physics and Astronomy at Iowa State University. Canfield’s research interests include the design, discovery, growth and characterization of novel electronic and magnetic compounds - often in single crystal form - and the study of their electrical, magnetic and thermal properties. Over the past three decades, he has helped discover, understand, and optimize materials with ferromagnetic and superconducting states as well as more exotic system that have fragile magnetism that can be manipulated so as to shed light on basic questions addressing the very origins of magnetic behavior. Canfield has received numerous awards and citations for his work. In 2020 he was elected to be a member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. In 2016, he was awarded the James C. McGroddy Prize for New Materials by the American Physical Society (APS). In 2015, he received a Humboldt Research Award. In 2014, he was awarded the David Adler Award in the field of Material Physics by the American Physical Society (APS) and was also placed on the Thomson Reuters’ World’s Most Influential Scientific Minds list. In 2011, Canfield received the Department of Energy Ernest Orlando Lawrence Award.
